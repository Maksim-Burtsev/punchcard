# 33. Systems Performance, 2nd ed. — Brendan Gregg

> Performance is not a property you can read off source code — it is the behaviour of one workload on one system, so the reviewer's leverage is over claims and over measurability, rarely over speed itself. Intuition about where the time goes is wrong often enough to be inadmissible: the bottleneck sits where the data says, and "this should be faster" is not a justification, it is a hypothesis nobody tested. The methodology runs from the workload and its latency inward — what is being asked of the system, how long each part of it takes, what fraction of the request the touched code actually held — never from whichever metric happened to be lying around, and never from a number improving for reasons no one can explain. The largest wins are almost never faster work; they are work deleted, cached, deferred, or batched out of existence, which is why elimination outranks acceleration and why a per-operation cost is only meaningful multiplied by its rate. And a change decides in advance whether the next investigation is possible at all: a diff that adds a resource nothing can see, or strips the symbols and events the profiler needs, has spent a future win to buy a present one.

## Principles

### 33.1 No performance claim without a measurement, and no tuning value without a mechanism
- **Why:** Performance is subjective until it is a number, and complex software always has many candidate issues — the work is identifying which one matters, which means quantifying the magnitude ("9 ms of a 10 ms request") rather than asserting a direction. Changing parameters until a metric improves is an anti-method: it produces settings nobody understands, may only mask a bug fixed later, and can behave worse at peak. A number with no explanation of what limited it is the start of an investigation, not its end — the book's own 3-4x "win" dissolved once the two sides were found to differ in CPU count, cache size, load level, and tenancy.
- **Applies:** Diffs whose stated motivation is performance with no before/after; tunables, pool sizes, buffer sizes, GC or compiler flags changed because "benchmarks improved"; config blocks traceable to a blog post; before/after numbers taken on different hardware, load, or data size; optimizations of code no profile showed hot; debug modes, stale pins, and mismatched sizes riding along in a config diff.
- **Unless:** Changes justified by correctness, clarity, or a known algorithmic ceiling owe no profile, and reverting to a default needs no measurement. Asynchronous background work legitimately fails to show in request latency. Where full analysis is unaffordable, an explicit list of unverified assumptions is an honest substitute for it.
- **Source:** Ch. 1.5, 1.6; 2.5.2, 2.5.17, 2.8.1; 5.1.1, 5.4.7; 12.1.3, 12.3.8; 16.1

### 33.2 Judge the change by the workload's latency, not by whatever metric is at hand
- **Why:** Time is the one quantity comparable across components and convertible into a speedup estimate; operation counts, IOPS, utilization, and hit ratios are not — 5x fewer operations means nothing if each grew 10x, and a busy CPU may be stalled rather than working. Request latency is the sum of time in every thread state, so a CPU profile silently omits the off-CPU majority of most workloads, and a device- or system-level proxy answers a different question than "what is the application blocked on".
- **Applies:** Perf claims backed only by CPU utilization, ops/sec, or ambient I/O-wait style metrics; optimizations of compute in paths dominated by I/O or lock waits; capacity math and SLOs expressed in raw op counts; new timers and spans whose captured interval and unit are not named (queue wait vs service time vs whole request; ms vs µs).
- **Unless:** Throughput is the right metric when the question is genuinely volume, and for code measured as CPU-bound the CPU profile is exactly the evidence. Blocked time must be attributed before it is optimized — much of what looks like waiting on the network or a lock is idle waiting for work.
- **Source:** Ch. 1.6; 2.3.1; 5.4.5; 6.3.7, 6.3.8; 8.3.1, 8.3.13; 9.3.1, 9.3.11

### 33.3 Report latency as a distribution; the mean of a bimodal system describes nothing
- **Why:** Systems latency is routinely multimodal — cache hit versus miss, fast path versus slow path — and the mean of two modes lands where almost no events are, the opposite of a central tendency. Occasional thousand-millisecond outliers vanish into an average that is dominated by hits at a 99% hit ratio, yet those outliers are what users feel and what investigations need; long averaging windows erase bursts the same way, turning a database blocked for hundreds of milliseconds into a dip.
- **Applies:** New metrics, SLO checks, dashboards, or test assertions built on a mean; latency instrumentation without buckets or percentiles; alerting windows longer than the bursts they must catch; read and write latency blended into one series; benchmark results quoted as a single number; averaging rates arithmetically when the harmonic mean is the correct one.
- **Unless:** Averages remain fine for capacity totals and trend lines, and not every quantity needs a histogram. Per-operation recording has real cost at high event rates — sampled or threshold-triggered capture is the legitimate compromise; a single high percentile is an improvement, not a full picture.
- **Source:** Ch. 2.8.2–2.8.6, 2.10; 5.5.7; 6.5.6, 6.7; 8.5.2, 8.5.4; 9.3.2; 10.5.4

### 33.4 Look for the work to delete before reviewing how fast the work runs
- **Why:** The remedy ladder runs don't do it, cache it, do it less, do it later, do it concurrently, and only then do it on faster hardware — elimination beats acceleration by an order of magnitude, and the book's biggest flame-graph wins are calls avoided entirely. Tuning is bounded by the cost of the path; deleting the calls is not. Fixing the caller can remove queries outright where tuning the storage layer only shaves a percentage off work that already paid the whole stack above it.
- **Applies:** Optimization diffs that speed up a path without asking whether its callers need it; recomputation of what could be memoized; eager per-request work that could be deferred until needed; per-request metadata checks, freshness probes, and repeated opens; sleep-and-recheck loops where a notification exists; redundant legacy processing kept alongside its replacement.
- **Unless:** Once the work is confirmed necessary, ordinary optimization is the right next step. Caches buy elimination with memory and staleness, deferral moves cost to a worse moment when the work is always needed, and some operations exist precisely to happen — never optimize away a durability write or a correctness check.
- **Source:** Ch. 2.3.4, 2.5.11, 2.5.20; 5.1.3, 5.2.2, 5.2.4, 5.2.8; 6.5.3, 6.9; 8.3.11, 8.8; 10.5.3

### 33.5 Price added work by its time-scale class times its rate, and by what its size grows with
- **Why:** Scaled to a one-second CPU cycle, memory access is minutes, an SSD read hours, a disk seek months, a cross-country round trip years — so one innocuous synchronous call inside a loop can cost more than every other line in the diff. The same wrapper is a rounding error at hundreds of events per second and a dominant regression at millions, which makes rate, not line count, the unit of overhead. Growth is the other half: an O(n²) scan or a per-connection buffer is fine until the population it tracks outgrows anything it has served, and then it dominates everything.
- **Applies:** Synchronous I/O, syscalls, IPC, or subprocess spawns inside per-item loops; a connection or client created per request instead of pooled; floods of tiny reads, writes, or sends where one batched operation would do; a new layer, validation pass, or serialization hop on a path whose event rate nobody states; nested scans over collections that grow with users or data; buffers, threads, and queues sized per connection with no expected connection count.
- **Unless:** Off the hot path a slow operation is fine, and over-strictness on cold paths is the mirror error — the table is instinct and triage, not a ban. Batching buys throughput with per-operation latency, so coalescing on a latency-sensitive path or across a durability point trades the wrong way, and big-O ignores constants where n is genuinely bounded and small.
- **Source:** Ch. 2.3.2; 3.2.2–3.2.3; 5.1.4, 5.2.1, 5.2.3, 5.5.5, 5.5.7; 9.3.6, 9.4.4; 10.3.5, 10.5.7; 11.2.2, 11.3.2

### 33.6 Bound every queue and every wait; degradation starts far below full utilization
- **Why:** Queueing makes latency grow nonlinearly well before a resource is saturated — for a disk-like model, mean response time doubles past 60% utilization and triples by 80%, with the tail degrading faster — so treating 100% as the threshold means acting after the damage. An unbounded queue is a latency defect wearing a safety margin: a full queue means every new entry waits behind all of it, and buffering piled into the middle of a path (bufferbloat) hides the backpressure that would have slowed the producer. A dependency that returns very slow successes is worse than one that fails, because nothing upstream can react.
- **Applies:** Unbounded in-memory queues, channels, or buffers on request paths; capacity bumps offered as a fix for drops; work-accepting APIs with no rejection path; retry loops with no cap feeding a saturated resource; new calls to storage, services, or peers with no timeout; capacity constants derived by dividing load by a peak benchmark number; efficiency-ordered work pickers with no age or deadline term.
- **Unless:** Sized buffering with its latency cost accepted is a legitimate throughput technique, and shedding is wrong for work that must not be dropped — then the shape to look for is a bound plus backpressure. Auto-sizing between clamps beats a fixed constant under variable load, and the exact knee is workload-dependent: batch systems can deliberately run hotter.
- **Source:** Ch. 2.3.9, 2.3.11–2.3.12, 2.6.5; 9.3.9, 9.4.1, 9.5.10; 10.3.6, 10.4.3; 12.3.7

### 33.7 Serialization and hold time cap what any amount of hardware can buy
- **Why:** Scalability is capped by the serial fraction and bent further by coherency cost, so one global lock, one single-threaded stage, or one shared mutable structure on a parallel path is an architecture problem no capacity fixes — the kernel keeps per-CPU run queues precisely because a single shared one would need synchronization on every operation. Hold time matters even when contention is not yet visible: slow work, I/O, allocation, or logging inside a critical section is the root cause that downstream waiters only reveal later, under load nobody has run yet.
- **Applies:** A global lock or single shared counter, map, or queue introduced on a hot concurrent path; widening an existing critical section; blocking calls made while holding a lock; a mutex taken per request or per dispatch; thread-per-blocking-request designs at high concurrency; removal of sharding or per-worker accumulation for simplicity; hot counters packed together without padding.
- **Unless:** Serialization is often necessary and cheap — the weight of the finding scales with how hot and how parallel the path is, and a simple global lock is right where contention is genuinely rare. Added parallelism is not free either: some workloads run faster on fewer threads once synchronization and lost locality outweigh it.
- **Source:** Ch. 2.3.8, 2.6.3–2.6.4; 5.2.5–5.2.6, 5.4.6; 6.2.3, 6.3.13; 7.2.8

### 33.8 A cache is justified by miss rate and miss cost, not by hit ratio
- **Why:** Total time is hit rate times hit latency plus miss rate times miss latency, and only the miss side is linear in the penalty — 98% versus 99% differs enormously while looking like one point, and a workload with a higher hit ratio can still issue more backing-store I/O than one with a lower ratio. Caches also start cold, so steady-state numbers say nothing about startup, and their memory is taken from somewhere: a cache sized past what the machine has costs far more in paging than the hits it wins.
- **Applies:** Caches added or resized on hit-ratio evidence alone, with no miss-cost accounting and no cold-start behaviour; two layers holding the same data; heap, buffer-pool, or cache limits derived from host memory inside a container or copied from a differently sized machine; eviction-policy changes with no workload rationale; claims about a cached path whose working set relative to memory nobody stated.
- **Unless:** Hit ratio is a fine health signal within one unchanged workload, and caching high in the stack is the right default because the hit is cheapest there. Testing or serving from the cached path is legitimate whenever that is the stated target — the requirement is that the claim match the working set.
- **Source:** Ch. 2.3.14, 2.5.18; 7.2.2, 7.2.10, 7.4.3; 8.3.12; 12.2.1

### 33.9 Leave behind the observability that would let this change's cost be measured
- **Why:** What is not deliberately exposed becomes an unknown-unknown nobody checks, and retrofitting visibility during an incident costs far more than building it in. Every resource a change introduces has a utilization, a saturation, and an error count; every service has a rate, an error rate, and a duration — and errors are the ones that hide, because recovered failures and silent retries accumulate latency while everything reports success. An opaque component that is ten percent faster loses to an observable one, since observability is what finds the eliminable work that dwarfs ten percent.
- **Applies:** Thread pools, connection pools, semaphores, queues, and limiters added with no busy time, backlog, or rejection counters; new services or endpoints emitting no rate/error/duration; retry, fallback, and correction logic with nothing counting how often it fires; build changes stripping symbols or frame pointers for a marginal gain; cross-service calls that drop the request identifier; production monitoring built by regexing another tool's human-readable output, or hooked into a dependency's private functions instead of a stable event.
- **Unless:** Instrument what can bottleneck, not every object; where USE-style metrics fit badly, latency analysis is the sanctioned alternative. Stripped binaries are fine with debuginfo shipped separately, an internal hook is acceptable when flagged as version-fragile and no stable event exists, and parsing text is pragmatic when it is the only interface — then the units deserve explicit validation.
- **Source:** Ch. 2.5.9–2.5.10; 4.1.2, 4.3.5, 4.6; 5.1.3, 5.4.4, 5.4.8, 5.6.1–5.6.2; 9.5.2; 10.6.1, 10.6.5

### 33.10 Size instrumentation to the event rate, and aggregate before crossing a boundary
- **Why:** Counters are effectively free while per-event work costs per event: the same probe is negligible at ten thousand events per second and harmful past a hundred thousand, and per-event tracing of a hot path has been measured slowing its target by an order of magnitude — the observer becomes the top consumer of what it reports. The discipline is to measure the rate cheaply first, aggregate into counts and histograms where the events happen, and ship only summaries; unfiltered raw-event export pays transfer cost for data that is about to be reduced anyway.
- **Applies:** Logging, metrics, spans, or callbacks added inside per-request, per-message, per-allocation, or per-iteration loops; instrumentation that formats or allocates before checking whether it is enabled; telemetry keyed on unbounded high-cardinality values instead of buckets; log or trace output written to the very resource being measured; timing maps keyed too coarsely, computing deltas without checking a start was recorded, or never deleting their entries.
- **Unless:** The book argues for more instrumentation, not less — the ask is that hot-path overhead be considered and ideally measured. Fixed-rate sampling has predictable cost by construction, low-frequency events (errors, opens, state changes) can afford per-event detail, and short bounded diagnostic sessions may be invasive on purpose.
- **Source:** Ch. 2.3.10; 4.2.1–4.2.3; 5.5.4; 7.4.1; 9.5.4; 10.5.6, 10.6.9; 14.3–14.4; 15.2.4

### 33.11 Benchmark code is reviewed for what it can actually reach, not for what it is named
- **Why:** The characteristic failure is measuring one thing, testing another, and concluding a third — famous "disk" benchmarks that issue no disk I/O because the working set fits in memory, write throughput measured without a sync, timing loops the compiler deleted because nothing consumed the result. Errors skew everything and are invisible in a latency number (a whole run once measured firewall-blocked clients timing out), and near saturation throughput scrapes upward while latency explodes, so a peak figure with no latency at that point is not a result.
- **Applies:** New perf tests and CI benchmark jobs; harnesses whose operation mix, sizes, concurrency, or configuration were invented rather than measured from production; single-threaded generators aimed at multi-core targets; runs shorter than a second, run once, reported as a single scalar; load tests that blend failed requests into the timings; changes to a perf test's I/O path, warmup, or timing method that silently break comparability with stored baselines; special-case fast paths exercised only by the benchmark.
- **Unless:** Micro-benchmarks are valuable exactly when their scope is stated and they are not sold as production predictions, and testing the cached or buffered path is legitimate when that is what production exercises. The defect is the mismatch between the claim and the mechanism, never the act of measuring.
- **Source:** Ch. 8.7; 9.8; 12.1.3, 12.2.1–12.2.3, 12.3.1–12.3.10

## Review heuristics

- For any diff whose stated reason is performance, find the measurement: which workload, taken where, and what fraction of end-to-end time the touched code held. No profile, no claim — and a tuning value with no explained mechanism is a guess even when the number improved.
- Take every new call that leaves the process, the CPU, or the language runtime, and multiply its cost by the real bound of the loop it sits in; that product, not the line count, is what the change costs.
- For each new collection, scan, buffer, thread, or connection, name what its size grows with — if the answer is users, rows, or traffic, a constant in review is a curve in production.
- Find every queue, channel, or buffer the change adds and name its capacity and its behaviour when full; unbounded means latency, not memory, is what breaks first, and every new outbound wait needs a bound too.
- When a cache is added or resized, ask for the miss rate and the miss cost, where the memory comes from, and what the path does cold — a hit ratio alone answers none of the three.
- For every pool, queue, limiter, or service the diff introduces, ask whether an outsider can see its busy fraction, its backlog, and its errors, including the failures it silently retries past.
- Weigh new logging, tracing, and metrics by events per second rather than by lines added: aggregate in place, keep high-cardinality keys out, and check the cost is near zero when disabled.
- For benchmark and perf-test code, confirm the operation it claims to measure can actually reach that layer, that errors are counted separately, and that any throughput figure carries the latency at that operating point.
