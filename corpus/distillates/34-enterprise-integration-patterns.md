# 34. Enterprise Integration Patterns — Hohpe, Woolf

> Integration is the craft of making separate systems cooperate without letting them depend on each other's insides, and the currency of that dependency is assumptions — about location, uptime, format, meaning, and order. Every assumption removed buys tolerance and costs infrastructure, so the reviewer's question is never "does this call work" but "which assumption does this new hop bake in, and who pays the day it stops holding". Across a boundary none of the guarantees a method call gives survive: messages arrive twice, out of order, far too late, or never; nothing a message merely points at is still reachable when it is read; no transaction spans two systems, so a failed step is compensated rather than rolled back; no debugger steps through the flow. Everything a single process got for free must now be written down — a contract, a correlation identifier, an idempotent receiver, a completeness condition, a destination for what cannot be processed. The catalog of patterns matters far less than that arithmetic: each one buys decoupling with another component, another channel, another hop, and another thing that can be stuck at 3 a.m.

## Principles

### 34.1 Judge a new integration by the assumptions it bakes in — starting with any interface that hides the network
- **Why:** Coupling is measured in assumptions: internal value representation, hardcoded location, both sides being up at once, fields identified by position. Each makes today's link cheaper and tomorrow's change breaking. An interface that makes a remote call look local hides all of them at once — the call is orders of magnitude slower, can fail mid-flight, and its counterparty changes on somebody else's schedule — so a caller written as if it were in-process turns one system's outage into everyone's. The same assumption runs in reverse when a producer knows each consumer by name: wiring every participant directly to every other grows connections quadratically and forces an edit into existing components each time one is added, so the decision of where a message goes next belongs in one place rather than in every producer or every step.
- **Applies:** Native or in-memory representations serialized onto the wire; hardcoded peer hostnames and URLs where a logical channel name belongs; consumers parsing by field offset; a new synchronous cross-service call on a path that could proceed without the answer; remote calls in loops, or with no timeout and no failure branch; a diff that adds an ordering dependency between two services' calls; a producer maintaining a registry of consumer callbacks, or an if/else picking among destination channels by business condition; a new service wiring direct calls to several existing ones; application-specific routing added to a shared engine other teams reuse.
- **Unless:** Loose coupling is not a panacea — every assumption removed adds infrastructure and a harder programming model. Inside one application, or one deployment released as a unit, local-call assumptions are correct and efficient; a user genuinely waiting for an answer wants a synchronous query, and one known callee with a caller that can block stays synchronous. A central router becomes a maintenance hotspot when the recipient set churns — subscriber-side registration is then the better inversion — and a mediator only helps while it stays stateless and scoped, or it is the new bottleneck.
- **Source:** Ch. 1 (Loose Coupling; One-Minute EAI); Ch. 2 (Remote Procedure Invocation; Messaging); Ch. 3 (Message Router); Ch. 7 (Message Broker); Ch. 13 (Architecture with Patterns; Structuring Channels)

### 34.2 A message crossing a team boundary is a published contract: sized to a business action, versioned in band, read tolerantly
- **Why:** Once another team consumes a format it is that application's public interface, and the expensive failures are semantic rather than syntactic — the same word meaning two different things in two systems. Granularity is the coupling dial: a message per field is chatty and re-couples consumers to the layout, a message bundling independent operations means you can never do one without the other. Producers and consumers never cut over at the same instant, so both formats are in flight together: the message says which one it is, and any rename, retype, or change of meaning is breaking until shown otherwise. No compiler stands behind any of this, so without tests and a version marker teams stop deleting anything and the schema goes add-only. The mirror-image defect is a consumer that binds strictly to the whole shape and breaks the day a field is added — read the fields you need, and validate hard the ones you act on.
- **Applies:** Internal or ORM models published straight onto a shared channel; one service reading or writing tables another owns; an event per attribute change, or one message fusing unrelated operations; units, cardinality, or inclusion rules changed on a live field with no version indicator and no consumer-impact note; a shared types package accreting fields one consumer uses; a schema change with no test exercising an existing consumer; fail-on-unknown-field deserialization in a cross-team or monitoring consumer; a consumer acting on a number or enum it never range-checks.
- **Unless:** Additive optional fields are exactly the tolerance loose coupling buys. With two or three participants the shared model may legitimately be one application's format, and messages between an application and its own translator are private. Inside one deployable, where producer and consumer version together, a strict contract catches more; and tolerant reading never extends to unvalidated values driving a decision.
- **Source:** Ch. 1 (Taking Orders; Change Address); Ch. 2 (Shared Database); Ch. 5 (Format Indicator); Ch. 8 (Canonical Data Model; Message Translator); Ch. 12 (Management Console — verifier states)

### 34.3 Build the payload for the wire: self-contained, minimal, with infrastructure fields in the envelope
- **Why:** A message is read in another process at another time, so anything it merely points at may be gone — an identifier the consumer must dereference forces a synchronous call back to the sender and throws away the decoupling the message was for, while serializing the whole object graph instead makes messages explode. One opaque field of language-native serialization is worse than both: routing, validation, and type-based dispatch all collapse because every message looks alike, and an incident means deciphering a blob. Publishing domain entities through a generic serializer does the same thing quietly — the message inherits constraints chosen for the domain, and the domain becomes hostage to the wire format. What routers and consumers select on has to sit in the header, where it is readable without parsing the body; what the recipient does not need should not be there at all, since whole-record payloads hand out identifiers and personal fields that were never the caller's business and invite intermediaries to depend on them. Bulk data belongs in a store behind a key minted for the purpose, and a missing field is filled by a step between the two ends rather than by widening the producer for one subscriber or sending the consumer into a third system.
- **Applies:** Payloads carrying ids the consumer is expected to dereference, or an entire aggregate serialized just in case; a single `data` field of native serialization; domain entities published straight through a serializer, or `toMessage`/broker annotations added to a domain class; routing, correlation, tracing, or dedup keys buried in the body; children of a split carrying only their own item with no parent context or identifier; a full record returned where the caller needs two fields; large blobs routed through stages that never read them; a lookup key that is the transport's own message ID; an event payload widened because one subscriber needs a field, or a producer calling a third service before publishing.
- **Unless:** Combining several small domain objects into one message is endorsed — it simplifies routing and cuts per-message overhead — and dedicated wire types a framework may serialize freely are the sanctioned middle ground. A claim check buys nothing when most stages need the data back, and its store needs a retention policy. An enricher and its source are inherently coupled, so a direct call there is fine, and a value computable from what is present or from the environment needs no source at all.
- **Source:** Ch. 7 (Splitter); Ch. 8 (Envelope Wrapper; Content Enricher; Content Filter; Claim Check); Ch. 10 (Messaging Mapper; Selective Consumer)

### 34.4 Design every receiver to survive the same message twice
- **Why:** Duplicates are structural, not exceptional — at-least-once delivery, lost acknowledgements, resend-until-acknowledged recovery, retries, failover, and replay-from-checkpoint all redeliver, and resend is often the cheapest recovery protocol available, but only if receivers tolerate it. A rerouting or replay path added after an outage is safe precisely and only because the receiver is idempotent. Either track message identity explicitly, with a deliberate history window and persistence choice, or define the operation so repetition is harmless — set the balance rather than add to it.
- **Applies:** Handlers whose effects are inserts, increments, charges, or outbound notifications with no dedup check; retry, resend-on-failover, or replay-since-last-healthy logic added upstream with no receiver-side guarantee; dedup windows sized without reference to the sender's in-flight count, or seen-id caches with no bound or eviction; duplicate defense implemented as a unique constraint on a business field, which breaks the day the business legitimately resends that key.
- **Unless:** Naturally idempotent operations — queries, absolute-value writes — need no machinery, and infrastructure dedup over them is pure throughput cost. Absolute semantics buy idempotency but do not by themselves settle concurrent conflicting updates. And where both ends are already idempotent receivers, the coarse remedy is the sanctioned one: reissue the requests parked in front of a failed service, or have the failover router resend everything received since that service was last confirmed healthy — the duplicate replies it produces are then harmless.
- **Source:** Ch. 10 (Idempotent Receiver); Ch. 4 (Guaranteed Delivery); Ch. 12 (Credit Bureau Failover — Limitations)

### 34.5 Never assume arrival order, and price what requiring it costs
- **Why:** A channel guarantees delivery, not delivery time: independently routed messages, retries, and parallel consumers all reorder, and asynchronous steps finish in whatever order they finish. Requiring order costs the parallelism the pipeline exists for — the reflex fix of acknowledging each item before releasing the next does guarantee order and simultaneously destroys the reason the work was distributed. The honest remedies are one consumer per ordered stream, or reordering at the receiving end behind a bounded buffer with a window that tells the producer how much room is left. Order also needs consecutive numbers assigned where the items are produced, in a field of their own: gaps are how a receiver detects what is missing, and identifiers minted for uniqueness are neither ordered nor consecutive.
- **Applies:** Consumers applying updates by arrival order across channels or partitions; replica count or consumer concurrency raised on an order-sensitive handler; a concurrency limit of one, or a per-item acknowledgement gating the next dispatch, introduced to fix an ordering bug; multi-part sends routed to a load-balanced pool with nothing pinning one sequence to one consumer; gap detection keyed on message IDs, UUIDs, or timestamps; reorder buffers with no capacity limit, no timeout on a gap, and a hardcoded start index or window size that only works while both ends restart together.
- **Unless:** Within one ordered partition consumed by one instance the guarantee is real. Where order does not matter, competing consumers are the cheap and correct scaling move. Windowed flow control needs influence over the producer, which you often lack at the receiving end of someone else's stream — there, buffering and reordering is all there is — and a stand-in for a missing item is legitimate when approximate data beats late data.
- **Source:** Introduction (Sequence issues); Ch. 3 (Pipes and Filters — Parallel Processing); Ch. 5 (Message Sequence); Ch. 7 (Resequencer — Sequence Numbers; Avoiding Buffer Overrun); Ch. 10 (Competing Consumers)

### 34.6 Correlate on a dedicated application-level identifier, carried in the header, minted by whoever multiplexes
- **Why:** An asynchronous caller with more than one request outstanding cannot match a reply to its cause, and matching by arrival order fails for the reason above. The identifier must be one the flow controls end to end: transport-assigned message IDs are re-minted by every intermediary that consumes and republishes — a router, a translator, a tap, a proxy — and some platforms do the opposite and copy one id onto every derived message, so it is not even unique. A business key welds two meanings onto one field. What the caller needs to resume is the business task, so carry the task's identity, in a header where infrastructure can route and filter without parsing bodies — and a proxy or gateway funnelling many callers onto one downstream channel must mint its own ID, store the caller's ID and reply address against it, and restore both on the way back.
- **Applies:** Reply handlers matching by order, timing, or content heuristics; correlation or idempotency keys taken from broker message IDs or delivery tags; correlation, reply-address, sequence, or version fields buried in the domain payload; an intermediary passing caller-chosen IDs straight through to a shared downstream channel, or republishing without copying correlation and tracing headers; a responder resolving the reply destination from its own configuration instead of from the request.
- **Unless:** One caller, no intermediaries, one outstanding request on a private reply channel makes the channel itself the correlation. Where a reply becomes the next request, the chain carries forward the identifier it arrived with — minting a fresh one at each hop pays only when someone actually needs to retrace the path step by step. When the responder only echoes transport IDs, a request-ID-to-task map is the right shape, not a smell; where the transport has no custom headers, body fields are the documented fallback.
- **Source:** Ch. 5 (Correlation Identifier; Return Address; Message Sequence); Ch. 9 (Managing Concurrent Auctions); Ch. 11 (Smart Proxy)

### 34.7 Every multi-step flow needs an owner for its state — completeness rule, expiry, instance identity, and a mechanism sized to the flow
- **Why:** Anything that waits — an aggregate, a pending-request table, a callback registry, a process instance, a dedup history — is state whose lifetime nobody bounded. A fan-in must answer three questions explicitly: which messages belong together, how it knows the set is complete, and how they combine. "Wait for all" is the slowest and most brittle answer, because one straggler stalls the batch and "missing" is itself hard to detect asynchronously. Entries added on send and removed only on reply leak forever, since lost replies are normal; purge too eagerly and a latecomer silently opens a fresh group under the same identity. A long-running coordinator runs many executions of one flow at once, so only an instance identifier it issued and the reply echoes says which one a message belongs to, and the current position cannot live in a worker that is step two and step four of the same flow. Size the mechanism to the flow: fixed wiring is cheapest and hardest to change, an itinerary travelling with the message handles a linear known sequence and loses the position when the message is lost, and a central coordinator buys branching, joins, and a queryable "where is this now" at the cost of a hub on every hop. Whichever it is, keep the fan-out encapsulated — if callers must know how many backends answer, the routing has leaked into every neighbour.
- **Applies:** Join or gather code whose completeness test is an assumed count, with no timeout path and no zero-reply case; maps keyed by correlation ID with no TTL, eviction, or sweep; callback registries holding caller context for replies that may never come; latecomers dropped with no record that information was lost; "current step" held in a singleton or per-component variable; a workflow engine, saga, or state machine introduced for a fixed three-step sequence, or conditional branches and retries accreting inside a static route list; each stage inspecting the payload to pick the next stage; callers awaiting several sub-results and merging them themselves, or an API whose shape reveals the number of backends; long-lived stateful workers with no way to list or clear in-flight state.
- **Unless:** Fire-and-forget fan-out needs no aggregator at all. Where an incomplete result is meaningless, wait-for-all plus an explicit timeout error is correct; storing only the fact of closure is cheap, so generous retention margins cost little. Branching, parallel steps with a join, or a need to query in-flight status are exactly what justifies a coordinator — and several scoped coordinators beat one that owns everything — but centralizing state means persisting after every step, which can make that store the new bottleneck.
- **Source:** Ch. 7 (Aggregator — Aggregation Strategies; Composed Message Processor; Scatter-Gather; Routing Slip; Process Manager); Ch. 9 (Aggregating Strategies); Ch. 10 (Messaging Gateway — pending request state)

### 34.8 Give every unprocessable message a named destination, and put something behind it
- **Why:** Some messages will never be processable, and all three reflexes are wrong: dropping destroys the evidence of an integration defect, requeueing to the same channel loops forever, and crashing means the next restart re-crashes on the same message. Give the case a destination — one channel for content this receiver cannot interpret, another for what the system could not deliver — preserve the original message's identity when republishing, and put something behind it that alerts, because an unwatched dead-letter channel is a silent drop with extra steps. Push-style delivery makes this worse than it looks: where the handler callback has no way to say "do not consume this", a runtime failure simply advances to the next message and the work is gone. Under sustained overload that channel grows until it takes the broker down with it, and its growth is a diagnostic: production is outrunning consumption upstream.
- **Applies:** Routers and consumer switches with no else branch; catch blocks that log-and-acknowledge a parse failure; push handlers whose only error path is a log line, or code assuming a thrown exception rolls the message back where the framework does not; retry loops with no poison-message escape; expirations added with no dead-letter consumer, alert, or retention bound; dead-letter handling that loses the original ID or provenance; purge tooling that deletes rather than quarantines for replay; a requester taking the next message off a durable reply channel without checking it correlates to its own request.
- **Unless:** A filter discarding uninteresting messages by design is not error handling. A well-formed message asking for something the domain refuses — delete a record that does not exist — is an application error and belongs in application error handling, not on the invalid channel. And a larger dead-letter queue never fixes a flow whose consumption is too slow.
- **Source:** Ch. 4 (Invalid Message Channel; Dead Letter Channel); Ch. 10 (Event-Driven Consumer); Ch. 11 (Channel Purger); Ch. 13 (Major Production Crash)

### 34.9 No transaction spans two systems — scope one unit, compensate instead of rolling back, and account for work already in flight
- **Why:** By default each send and each receive commits alone, so a crash between "consumed the message" and "wrote the row / sent the next message" loses work silently, and a component that consumes one message and emits several can leave half its recipients notified. The scoped remedies are known: one transaction covering receive-and-send or receive-and-write, so the input stays on the channel until the whole unit commits; persisted progress so a restart resumes with what is left; or resend everything and rely on idempotent receivers. Beyond one unit there is no rollback to have: a business activity assembled over days or across services is a sequence of separate committed steps, and a failure late in it triggers compensating messages that undo the effects while preserving the history. One shape is always a bug — sending a request and awaiting its reply inside one transaction deadlocks, because the request is not visible to anyone until commit. And rerouting away from a failed dependency only protects new work: everything already queued in front of it stays stuck, so the switch owes a resend — reissue what is parked there, or replay everything received since that dependency was last confirmed healthy.
- **Applies:** Consume-then-produce and consume-then-write handlers on auto-acknowledge; fan-out loops acknowledging the input before every output is durably sent, with no transaction, outbox, or dedup story; a transactional send followed by a blocking receive of the correlated reply; a multi-step orchestration whose steps have no compensating action, or an error path assuming the whole flow can simply be undone; two-phase commit introduced across service boundaries or around user think-time; a failover switch, breaker, or endpoint reroute with no story for the requests already parked at the dead endpoint.
- **Unless:** Push consumers often cannot control the transaction at all — know which side commits before assuming a retry exists. Short-lived operations over resources you own do map onto classic distributed transactions, and there the atomic route is simpler. Genuinely idempotent or loss-tolerant flows can skip the machinery, provided that is a decision rather than an omission.
- **Source:** Ch. 10 (Transactional Client — Send-Receive Message Pairs; Event-Driven Consumer); Ch. 7 (Recipient List — Robustness); Ch. 12 (Credit Bureau Failover); Ch. 14 (long-running activities and compensation)

### 34.10 Confine transport knowledge to one endpoint layer that speaks the domain, can be faked, and does not reimplement the platform
- **Why:** Messaging APIs spread through business code make every infrastructure change — a broker swap, a client upgrade, a move off messaging entirely — a codebase-wide edit, and make the logic untestable without a live broker. A gateway exposing domain operations instead of send-and-receive keeps that knowledge in one place, and the abstraction is only real if vendor exception types are translated at the same boundary; otherwise callers import the library anyway. Mapping between domain objects and messages belongs in a component both sides are ignorant of, so a domain class is not edited by every integration change. The seam pays twice: a fake implementation can invoke the reply path directly from the request call, collapsing an asynchronous round-trip into one debuggable thread. It also sorts errors correctly — what the endpoint cannot parse is an invalid message, what the service rejects is an application error — and it is where an easily-forgotten protocol step (acknowledge, re-arm, close) is owned once instead of being remembered in every handler. What the seam must not do is grow a private reimplementation of delivery guarantees, ordering, or identity that the platform already provides.
- **Applies:** Broker SDK imports, channel names, acknowledgement or serialization handling inside domain modules; vendor error classes named in catch blocks above the integration layer; business logic implemented inside a message handler so no other entry point can reach it; a second protocol added by copying the first handler; several consumers repeating the same setup and teardown lines around different bodies; hand-rolled acknowledgement, resend, sequence-number, dedup, or service-to-service token schemes layered over a transport that already offers them; components testable only against a running broker, or tests that sleep and poll real queues.
- **Unless:** Inside the gateway or adapter layer, raw transport code is the point, and a two-layer chain — transport syntax below, domain vocabulary above — is often better than one. Do not build the seam speculatively for a one-off script, and extract shared scaffolding once two components actually share the shape. The fake replaces the real gateway's code, so the real one still needs tests of its own; and where a platform mechanism is immature or contested, implementing the pattern directly is the honest answer.
- **Source:** Ch. 3 (Message Endpoint); Ch. 9 (A Few Words on Testing); Ch. 10 (Messaging Gateway; Messaging Mapper; Service Activator); Ch. 14 (reliability and security standards)

### 34.11 Adding a consumer changes the contract — delivery semantics, subscription identity, and consumption rate are explicit decisions
- **Why:** Exactly-one-consumer delivery and broadcast are different contracts, and the mistakes are symmetric: a second logical consumer on a work queue silently steals messages, while extra instances on a broadcast subscription each process everything and duplicate every side effect, so parallelism there has to come from inside one consumer. Observation belongs on a tap that republishes, never on the working channel. A subscription that must survive a disconnect needs a stable identity and an unsubscribe path — an inactive durable subscription accumulates without limit, and anything presenting the same identity inherits the backlog, including a different application. Rate is the third decision: push delivery processes as fast as messages arrive unless the number of concurrent handlers is the cap, polling throttles by construction, and a worker spawned per message turns a traffic spike into a dead machine. When the consumer still falls behind, tuning the producer's interval leaves the wrong party in control.
- **Applies:** A logging, audit, or monitoring consumer attached directly to a work queue, or a channel flipped to broadcast to enable it; replicas added to a broadcast subscriber to "share the load"; a queue sharded by hand to get parallelism; a durable subscription with a generated client id or subscription name, no retention cap, and no teardown; new push subscribers with no concurrency cap or prefetch bound; a thread, process, or task per message; busy-poll loops with no blocking wait; consumer-side filters split into ranges that no longer cover the domain, or filtering used as if it were access control; debounce or sampling added to a stream of partial updates; performance changes argued from average latency with no evidence of where messages actually queue.
- **Unless:** Competing instances of one logical consumer are exactly what a point-to-point channel is for, and where a backlog is genuine and capacity exists, adding them is the right answer. Push is more efficient than polling for sparse traffic, and pull costs a cycle of latency that is wrong where immediate forwarding is the requirement. Broker filtering beats receiving and discarding, but criteria too rich for its language legitimately live in code; dropping is safe for full snapshots and loses fields when applied to deltas; and coalescing only pays when related messages genuinely cluster.
- **Source:** Ch. 4 (Point-to-Point Channel; Publish-Subscribe Channel); Ch. 10 (Competing Consumers; Durable Subscriber; Selective Consumer; Polling and Event-Driven Consumer); Ch. 11 (Wire Tap); Ch. 13 (Flashing Market Data Updates; Major Production Crash)

### 34.12 Build in the means to see a flow nobody can step through
- **Why:** Loose coupling's deliberate ignorance of who talks to whom makes the whole picture unknowable — the book's own complaint is that it becomes "impossible to understand in which direction messages actually flow" — and no debugger attaches to it. So provenance travels in the message: a path record in the header is also what stops a component that both publishes and subscribes to one channel from feeding itself an infinite loop. Liveness is not health either, since a component emits perfect heartbeats while garbling its output; probe with synthetic messages through the real processing path and range-check the answers, not merely that something came back. Mark that traffic out of band — a sentinel in a business field is one more field with two meanings. Report transitions rather than steady state, with a startup announcement so silence is readable, and put status, metrics, and control commands on their own channel with their own validation and priority, since a reset stuck behind a business backlog never arrives.
- **Applies:** New channels, routers, or hops with no tracing, correlation metadata, or topology inventory; a component gaining both a publisher and a subscriber on one channel with no origin check; health checks that ping a port for a component whose failure mode is wrong output; synthetic checks that verify arrival but not content or latency; reserved IDs or magic values marking test traffic; health, metric, or failover messages published onto business channels; a metric or audit message emitted per business message on a hot path; alerting that fires on every poll and never on recovery; expensive validation hard-wired into the hot path with no runtime toggle.
- **Unless:** For a small stable topology a documented channel list is enough — the obligation scales with how much indirection the change adds. Probes cost load and sometimes money per call, and against a stateful system they leave test records in real reports. Interval summaries beat per-message instrumentation, per-message detail is affordable only on low-volume high-value flows, and a tap's copies carry their own ids and timestamps, so measurements must be based on those.
- **Source:** Ch. 3 (Message Router — drawbacks); Ch. 11 (Message History; Wire Tap; Control Bus; Test Message; Smart Proxy); Ch. 12 (Verify the Credit Bureau Operation; Management Console)

## Review heuristics

- For every message schema the diff adds or changes, ask who outside this repository parses it, what tells a consumer which version it received, and whether code deployed yesterday still works after the change lands.
- Read the new payload as the receiver will: anything it must dereference, anything opaque, anything sensitive it did not ask for, and any routing or correlation key sitting in the body instead of the header.
- Take any handler with a side effect and deliver its message twice on paper: if the second delivery inserts, increments, charges, or notifies again, name the dedup identity or the absolute-value semantics that makes it safe.
- Find every place the change pairs a response to a request and confirm the match runs on a dedicated correlation field carried end to end — not arrival order, not a broker-assigned ID, not a business key — then, for whatever state that key accumulates (aggregates, pending callbacks, dedup histories, process instances), locate the eviction path and the behaviour when the awaited message never arrives.
- Follow one message the consumer cannot process: where it lands, what takes it out of the retry loop, who is alerted, whether its original identity survives, and what would drain that channel during an overload.
- Check each consume-then-produce or consume-then-write step for what commits when; an acknowledgement before the downstream effect is durable is lost work, a send that awaits its own reply inside the transaction never completes, and a multi-step flow with no compensating action has no error path at all.
- Wherever the change adds consumers, replicas, subscriptions, or concurrency, make it state the price: ordering across the group, duplicated side effects if the channel broadcasts, who owns the subscription identity, and what now bounds the consumption rate.
- Ask how anyone answers "where is this request right now" after the change — message provenance, a tap, or a queryable store — because nothing in this flow can be stepped through.
